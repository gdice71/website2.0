Biomechanics Research

Knee Osteoarthritis Lubrication CFD Study

This in-progress research project asks a mechanical question with clear clinical consequences: as osteoarthritis deforms the knee joint, how does that altered geometry change synovial fluid flow and lubrication performance? The aim is to move past generic descriptions of cartilage wear and quantify when the joint starts failing mechanically.

Using MRI-derived patient geometry across multiple Kellgren-Lawrence grades and BMI ranges, I am building 3D models of bone, cartilage, meniscus, synovial fluid, and membrane, then running CFD to compare pressure distribution, wall shear stress, flow velocity profiles, and likely lubrication breakdown points.

Question How osteoarthritic deformation changes joint lubrication mechanics
Methods MRI segmentation, 3D reconstruction, CFD, comparative cohort analysis
Status In progress with segmentation review, fluid-domain preparation, and phased CFD runs

Technical direction

The workflow starts with MRI data and segmentation. Bone and cartilage masks come from the OAIZIB-CM dataset and are manually reviewed at articular surfaces where boundary accuracy matters most for CFD. Synovial fluid is segmented manually from proton-density fat-saturated MRI so the fluid domain reflects the actual joint space rather than an idealized gap.

The comparison axes are disease severity and BMI. Age and sex will be handled as acknowledged limitations rather than perfectly controlled variables, which is the more defensible approach at this cohort size. If needed computationally, the mid-BMI column stands on its own as a publishable progression study before extending to lean and high-BMI cases.

Current outputs

  • Pressure distribution in the synovial fluid domain
  • Wall shear stress on cartilage surfaces
  • Fluid velocity profiles through the joint space
  • Candidate lubrication breakdown regions across OA stages

In plain language

A healthy knee depends on a thin layer of synovial fluid to help the cartilage surfaces slide smoothly against each other. This project looks at whether osteoarthritis changes the joint shape enough that the fluid can no longer spread pressure properly or protect the cartilage the way it should.

In simpler terms, I am testing whether worn joints stop lubricating themselves effectively before total failure is obvious on a scan. That could help explain why some knees suddenly become much more painful or mechanically unstable, and why timing matters when deciding on treatment or surgery.

Model components

  • Femur and femoral cartilage
  • Tibia with medial and lateral tibial cartilage
  • Meniscus
  • Synovial fluid and synovial membrane
  • Ligaments omitted to avoid unreliable segmentation noise

Why the angle is novel

Most discussions of osteoarthritis stay descriptive: cartilage thins, bone changes, symptoms worsen. This project treats the knee as a mechanical fluid-lubrication system and asks how real patient geometry changes that system over time. To my knowledge, there is limited computational work comparing multiple disease stages and BMI ranges with patient-specific geometry in this exact way.
